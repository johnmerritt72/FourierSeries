What is a Fourier series?

Picture a dot moving around a circle at a steady speed. If you only watch how far left or right it is, it slides back and forth smoothly. That back-and-forth motion is called a cosine wave. If you only watch how high or low it is, you get a sine wave, which is the same wave started a quarter-turn later.

Now stick a second, smaller circle on the edge of the first one, and make it spin faster. Then add a third circle on the edge of the second. The dot at the end of this chain moves in a much more complicated way than any single circle could. A Fourier series is simply a list of these circles: how big each one is, how fast it spins, and where it starts.

The French mathematician Joseph Fourier (1768–1830) discovered something surprising: if you add up enough of these waves, you can build almost any pattern that repeats, even patterns with sharp corners. The picture below builds a "square wave" (a pattern that jumps between two flat levels) out of smooth sine waves.

Each wave in the list is called a term. Term number k spins k times while the first term spins once. So term 1 is the big, slow circle and term 200 is a tiny, fast one. N on this page is the highest term being used.

How circles can draw any shape

Every point in a drawing has two numbers: x (how far right) and y (how far up). Imagine tracing a closed shape with your finger, from the starting point all the way around and back again. As you go, x goes up and down and ends where it started, and so does y. Both are repeating patterns, and a Fourier series can build any repeating pattern.

So the page builds two Fourier series: one for x and one for y. That's why you see two chains of circles. The teal chain at the top controls left-right: the dashed line drops straight down from its tip. The orange chain on the left controls up-down: its dashed line goes straight across. Where the two lines meet is the pen.

Press Single chain to see the same drawing made by one chain of circles centred on the shape, with the pen right at its tip. This works because each term's x-wave and y-wave can be combined and split into two circles of the same speed spinning in opposite directions (one clockwise, one counter-clockwise). The circles are different, but where the tip goes is exactly the same, so the drawing and the error don't change. The biggest circles are placed first.

With only one term, each chain is a single circle and the pen draws a plain oval. Each new term adds a smaller, faster circle that fixes finer details. As N grows, the drawing gets closer and closer to the real shape, and the error number shrinks toward zero. This works for any shape you can draw without lifting your pencil, which mathematicians call a continuous curve.

Why some shapes need more terms

Smooth shapes like the heart look nearly perfect after a few dozen terms. Shapes full of sharp corners, like the Moore curve, need many more. A sharp corner is a sudden change of direction, and only small, fast-spinning circles can change direction quickly. The Moore curve has more than a hundred corners, so even with 255 terms it is still about half a percent off.

If a shape had a real gap, where the pen had to jump, the series would always overshoot the jump by about 9%, no matter how many terms you used. This is called the Gibbs phenomenon. This page avoids it by always turning your shape into one connected loop.

How this page finds the terms

Step 1: Turn the shape into a loop of points

Built-in shapes are lists of corner points connected by straight lines. For an SVG file, the page asks the browser for about 6,000 evenly spaced points along its paths. For a picture, it traces the outline (see Tracing your images).

Step 2: Walk around the loop at a steady speed

The page uses a "clock" called t that goes from 0 to one full turn. In math, one full turn is 2π, about 6.28. As t ticks forward, the pen walks around the loop at a steady speed, so at every moment we know x(t) and y(t).

Step 3: Measure how much of each wave is inside

To find out how much of the wave cos 3t is hiding in x(t), multiply the two together at every moment and take the average:

  • If x(t) rises and falls in step with the wave, the products are mostly positive, so the average is big.
  • If they don't match, the positive and negative products cancel out, so the average is close to zero.

Double that average and you have the number in front of cos 3t. The same trick with sin 3t gives the other number. Repeat for every term, for both x and y. The plain average of x(t) is the center of the shape, which is the first number in each formula in Show series.

A shortcut: only the corners matter

Every shape on this page is made of straight line pieces. Along a straight piece, the pen moves in the same direction at the same speed, so nothing interesting happens there. Everything interesting happens at the corners, where the direction changes.

Using some calculus, the "multiply and average" step can be rewritten as a sum over the corners. Each corner adds (how much the pen's speed changes at that corner) × (where the wave is at that moment), and the total is divided by k². So instead of averaging thousands of moments, the page adds up one number per corner, and the answer is exact, not an estimate.

The k² also explains why the circles shrink so fast: term 10 is roughly 100 times smaller than term 1.

Step 4: Turn each pair of numbers into a circle

Each term has two numbers, a for cosine and b for sine. Together, a cos kt + b sin kt is exactly one circle. Its radius comes from the Pythagorean theorem, r = √(a² + b²), and a and b also set its starting angle. These are the circles you see spinning.

Step 5: Check the result

The page compares the drawing with the real shape at 2,048 moments around the loop. The error is the farthest the drawing ever gets from the real shape, as a percentage of the shape's longer side.

Why are some terms zero?

For the Moore curve, the second half of the loop is the first half turned upside down (rotated 180°). An even-numbered wave (k = 2, 4, 6, …) looks exactly the same in the second half of the loop as in the first, but the shape is upside-down there, so the two halves cancel out to exactly zero. The page notices this and skips the even terms. The square and the Koch snowflake have the same kind of symmetry.

Tracing your images

When you upload a picture (PNG, JPG and so on), the page finds the outline in a few steps:

  1. Shrink the picture so its longer side is at most 320 pixels. That's plenty of detail and keeps things fast.
  2. Decide which pixels are the shape. If the picture has a transparent background, anything that isn't see-through counts. Otherwise, pixels darker than the threshold count. The starting threshold is chosen with Otsu's method, which picks the brightness that best splits the picture into a dark group and a light group. If the picture's edges end up counted as "shape", the page assumes it's a light drawing on a dark background and flips the choice.
  3. Follow the edges. The page walks along the border between shape pixels and background pixels until it gets back to where it started. That gives one closed outline. It repeats this for every outline, including holes such as the middle of an "O".
  4. Smooth the stair-step edges by averaging each point with its neighbors.
  5. Join the pieces. One chain of circles can only draw one loop, so the page connects each separate outline to the nearest point of the drawing so far with a thin "bridge" that the pen travels out and back along. Tiny specks are ignored.
  6. Space the points evenly (1,024 of them) and find 255 terms for each of x and y.

Tracing works best on clean pictures: logos, icons, silhouettes and simple drawings. Photos usually have too many shades to give a useful outline. SVG files skip tracing entirely and are usually the best choice.
